Start here

Get one account. ChatGPT or Claude, either one, about twenty dollars a month. The free versions are real but slower and more limited, and the paid one is the difference between a novelty and an assistant. Pick one and stay with it for a month rather than sampling four.

Then learn the only skill that actually matters: give it context. Most people type six words, get something generic back, and decide the tool is overrated. Tell it who you are, who it is for, how long it should be, and what you want it to sound like. The difference is not subtle.

✗Too little to go on
Write a newsletter about our meeting.
✓The same ask, with context
I am the associational mission strategist for an association of 22 churches in rural Kansas. Write a short monthly newsletter update, about 300 words, warm and plain, no hype. Here are my notes: [paste your bullet points]. Most of my readers are bivocational pastors who will read this on a phone.

Level 1. Search

This is asking it a question instead of searching the web, and handing it something long instead of reading all of it. It is the lowest possible risk and the easiest place to start.

Read this so I do not have to
Here is a 40 page document. Give me one page a bivocational pastor could read in 20 minutes. Plain language. Tell me what it asks him to actually do.
Get me ready for this meeting
I am meeting with a pastor whose church has declined from 120 to 35 over 15 years in a farming town losing population. Give me eight honest questions to ask him that are not insulting, and three things I should be careful not to say.
Tell me about this town
Tell me what is publicly known about the population trend, main employers, and age makeup of [county], Kansas over the last 20 years, and what that would likely mean for a small church there.

Level 2. Communicate

This is using it to write the thing that has been sitting undone. It gets you to about seventy percent in under a minute. The last thirty percent is still yours, and it always will be.

Write up my visit
Here is a voice memo I recorded leaving a church visit. Turn it into a visit note with the date, who I met, what we discussed, and a separate list of anything I said I would do.
Draft my newsletter
Turn these four bullet points into a warm 300 word update for our churches. Do not use exclamation points. Sound like a pastor, not a marketing department.
Write the email I am avoiding
Help me write a kind and clear email to a pastor who did not follow through on something he committed to. Direct, not harsh. I want to keep the relationship.
Turn this recording into minutes
Here is the recording of our committee meeting. Give me clean minutes, then a separate list of decisions made and who owns each follow up.

Level 3. Build

This is making something that did not exist before. It is the level most people assume they cannot reach, and it is the one that pays back the most, because you build it once and it serves people for years.

Make a good thing reproducible. Our church runs a free neighborhood sports camp every June. Other pastors kept asking how we do it, and I kept re-explaining it. So I had everything we know turned into one document another church could actually pick up and run: schedule, budget, volunteer structure, timeline.

You have something your association does well. Somebody in another association is going to ask you about it. Make it once instead of explaining it eleven times.

Turn a pile into one page. Every potential church planter I work with goes through a pre-application packet. It was a stack of PDFs nobody could hold in their head. I had the whole stack read and synthesized, the folder reorganized, and this made: one picture a man can look at once and finally understand what he is walking into.

Yours might be the constitution, the bylaws, four years of minutes and the personnel policy. Right now that is a drawer. It could be one page a search committee could actually read.

Turn training into a link, and tools into a place people can find them. Material you already teach can become a page you text to a pastor instead of a binder you mail him. Both of these are free, both work on a phone, and neither one required knowing how to write code.

For your seat at the table

Associational mission strategists

The newsletter, the annual calendar, the visit notes from a two hour drive, the report to the state office, the ordination council question set, the county demographic picture, the forty page document condensed for a man with twenty minutes. None of this touches the part of your work that is actually yours.

Webster Conference Center

Confirmation emails that sound like a person wrote them, packing lists by age group, retreat schedules, summer staff onboarding material, dietary and food count summaries, work order logs, and honest description copy for the families and civic groups who have never been out there.

Convention office and support staff

Minutes from a recording, form letters that do not read like form letters, scholarship applications summarized side by side, expense categorization, a spreadsheet turned into a chart for a board report, and the first draft of anything you have been dreading.

Two rules

Always read everything it makes.

It will get you most of the way there and hand it to you with complete confidence, including when it is wrong. It will invent a date, a statistic, or a Scripture reference that sounds right and is not. It does not know it is wrong, because it does not know anything.

So read every word before it goes out with your name on it. Check names, numbers, dates and Scripture. Then fix the voice, because it does not talk like you and your people know how you talk. It is a very fast typist with no discernment. You are still the one with discernment.

Keep people's private things out of it.

You handle what nobody else handles. A pastor's marriage, a moral failure, a church fight, a personnel file. Do not type that in. Not because something terrible will certainly happen, but because it is not yours to hand to a company.

The practical version: take the names out. "A pastor in a church of forty is dealing with a conflict between two deacons" gets you the same help and costs you nothing.

Your next step

One task. Not three. Pick the smallest real thing on your desk and hand it over this month.

Then two habits, and if you get these you do not need anybody's training session. When you learn something useful, tell somebody. Call the strategist two counties over and show him. And when you hear that somebody else is trying something, go try it yourself. Try it badly. Ask for help out loud, from somebody younger than you if that is who knows. There is no shame in that room.

The point was never speed. The point is the hour it gives back, and the pastor in a town of nine hundred who has not had anybody ask how he is doing since March.
